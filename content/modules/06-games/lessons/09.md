## Lesson objectives
By the end, the student can…
- Keep a **settings table** for every number that shapes the game.
- Collect playtest numbers and spot problems (a level nobody beats, a strategy that always wins).
- Change one number at a time and prove it helped.

## Words for today
- **Balance** — making a game fair and fun: no part too easy, too hard or too random.
- **Settings table** — a list of every tuning number and what it does.
- **Playtest data** — numbers from real players (scores, time, where they failed).
- **Random (luck)** — things the player can't control. A little is fun; too much feels unfair.
- **Dominant strategy** — one way of playing that always wins, so nobody tries anything else.

## 1. Warm-up — Something's off (10 min · 1-on-1: 5 min)
Play an unbalanced version of the demo: one power-up makes you win every time, and level 2 is impossible. What's wrong? How would you fix it?

```solo
Play it together. Each of you names the one number you'd change first. Same answer?
```

## 2. Mini-lesson — Balancing like a designer (12 min · 1-on-1: 8 min)
Show **Visual 1**:
1. **Settings table:** every number in one place (speed, spawn rate, lives, points, power-up time).
2. **Collect numbers:** for each playtest, save level reached, score, time, and where they lost.
3. **Spot the problem:** a level almost nobody passes? A power-up everyone uses and always wins with? Rounds that are over in 5 seconds?
4. **Change ONE number,** play again, compare.

**Good randomness:** where things appear, small surprises. **Bad randomness:** losing because of pure luck, with nothing you could do.

## 3. Hands-on build — Measure and tune (43 min · 1-on-1: 30 min)
1. Ask Claude Code for a stats log:
```
Add a simple stats log to my game, saved on the device only: for each round save the date, level reached, score, seconds played and what made the player lose. Add a hidden "Stats" screen (open it from Settings) that shows the last 20 rounds and lets me copy them as a table. Also move every tuning number into one settings list if it isn't already.
```
2. Make your settings table in Sheets.
3. Get 3 or more people to play 2 rounds each (or play 6 rounds yourself in a pinch). Copy the stats into Sheets.
4. Ask Claude: "Here are my playtest stats and settings: [paste]. What do you notice? Which ONE setting would you change first, and why?" Then decide yourself.
5. Change one number, run 3 more rounds, compare.

**Checkpoint 1 (+20 XP):** stats log + settings table + 6 or more rounds of data.
**Checkpoint 2 (+20 XP):** one change made and measured, with a before-and-after number.

```group
Pairs play each other's games to add data.
```

```solo
You be 2 different players: one careful, one reckless. Do both play styles get a fair shot?
```

> 🛡️ **Data moment:** the stats log stays on the device and holds no names. Only collect what helps you improve the game.

## 4. Game / challenge — Predict the Change (10 min · 1-on-1: 7 min)
Before changing a setting, write your guess: "If I make the bugs 20% slower, the average level will go from 2 to 3." Then test. Were you close?

```group
Closest prediction: +15 XP. Everyone who tests a prediction: +5.
```

```solo
**Student vs. Bolt.** You both predict the effect of the same change, then test it. Closest guess wins. +15 XP for playing, +5 for the closer guess.
```

## 5. Share-out — The number that mattered (10 min · 1-on-1: 5 min)
Share one setting, its old and new value, and the before-and-after numbers.

```solo
The student presents their change like a sports stat: "Before: ___. After: ___."
```

## 6. Exit ticket (5 min)
1. What's a dominant strategy, and why is it a problem?
2. What's good randomness vs. bad randomness?
3. Which setting did you change, and what happened?

```solo
Ask out loud. Keep the settings table updated from now on.
```

## Differentiation
- **Extra help:** track just score and level, and change one setting.
- **Extra challenge:** make an "easy / normal / hard" setting that changes several numbers at once, and test that each feels right.
- **1-on-1 tip:** turn tuning into a game: who can guess the effect of a change better, you or the student?
